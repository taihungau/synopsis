01The whole thing in seven sentences

If you read nothing else, read this page.

1. Artificial intelligence runs on specialised chips called GPUs. Every AI product needs them, by the thousand, running day and night.

2. The world is desperately short of them. The four biggest technology companies alone committed roughly $725 billion to building this capacity in 2026 — up 77% in a single year.1 Only $193.7 billion of chips were actually delivered against that.2

3. The shortage cannot be fixed quickly. Demand for AI computing grew sevenfold in a year. The capacity to serve it grew 36%.34 Connecting a new data centre to the electricity grid takes five to ten years.6

4. So prices are chaos. The identical chip rents for anywhere between $1.49 and $6.98 an hour on the same day, depending only on who you ask.5 A barrel of oil moves 0.01% between sellers, because oil has an exchange. Compute does not.

5. Exascale is that exchange. We do not buy chips or own buildings. We run the marketplace where owners and users meet, and keep a slice of everything that crosses it.

6. Both sides are already signed. $137 million of supply contracted across 13 data centres; $1.34 billion of demand under signed letters of intent across 214 named accounts.OURS Demand is 9.8 times the supply we have secured.

7. The company is raising $10 million, of which $1.72 million is already wired. Your $5 million would be most of what remains.

Two things to hold in your head while you read

Signed is not the same as banked. The $1.34 billion is under letters of intent, which are statements of intent to buy, not binding orders. The deck says so explicitly: demand is "under signed LOI, not booked." No trade has yet crossed the exchange. Treat every forward figure in section 08 as a plan, not a result.

Figures marked OURS are Exascale's own. Figures with a numbered chip come from an outside party you can check. That distinction is the most useful habit you can bring to this document, and it is the company's own convention.

02Six words you will hear

The only vocabulary you need.

The rest of this document avoids jargon. These six are unavoidable, so here they are once.

GPU
A specialised computer chip. The engine artificial intelligence runs on. A very expensive, very fast electric motor.
GPU-hour
One chip running for one hour. The unit that gets bought and sold, the way oil is sold by the barrel.
Exchange
A marketplace with one public price where buyers and sellers meet. A stock exchange, but for computing power.
Take rate
The small percentage the marketplace keeps from each transaction. How we get paid.
Basis point
One hundredth of one percent. "180 basis points" means 1.8%. Bankers count this way; it means nothing more.
LOI
Letter of intent. A signed statement that a customer intends to buy. Serious, but not yet an order.
03Supply and demand

Demand multiplies. Supply adds. That gap is the business.

This is the single most important idea in the document. Demand for AI computing grows in multiples — several times over, each year. Supply grows in percentages. The two lines cannot meet.

In one year, the number of AI requests handled at Google alone rose sevenfold.3 Over the same year, the amount of usable data-centre capacity in North America grew 36%.4 Not 36% short of demand — 36% growth against 600% growth.

Why supply cannot simply catch up

Building this capacity is slow and physical, and every step has its own queue. These are the lead times on adding a single megawatt of new capacity:46

What has to happenHow long it takes
Get the chips allocated to you3–6 months
Build the shell and fit it out12–24 months
Obtain a power transformer2–4 years
Connect to the electricity grid5–10 years

There are 2,600 gigawatts of generation sitting in United States grid connection queues,6 and the country is short 439,000 construction workers to build it — 300,000 of them electricians.7 Vacancy across primary data-centre markets is 1.4%. In Northern Virginia it is 0.3%.4

And the capacity that already exists is not for sale

Every GPU cloud sells only its own racks. Nobody sells everybody's. So an operator with idle chips on a Tuesday has no way to sell that Tuesday to someone else's customer, while that customer waits months for capacity that is sitting unused a state away.

04The gap

The same chip, the same day, at five times the price.

This one fact is the reason the company exists. If you remember a single chart, remember this.

A survey of more than fifteen providers found the identical chip — an NVIDIA H100 — quoted between $1.49 and $6.98 an hour on the same day.5 For comparison, a barrel of Brent crude moves about 0.01% between sellers, because oil has had an exchange since 1988.

Every commodity in history eventually got an exchange, and each one arrived when the same three things became true together: the unit became standard, supply spread out across many sellers, and the price started moving faster than contracts could be renegotiated. All three are now true of computing power. The H100 price fell 79%, then rose 38% in five months.8 Nobody could hedge a dollar of that.

How to read "9.8 times more demand than supply"

It is a real signal that the shortage is genuine, and it is the best evidence in the deck. It is not revenue waiting to be collected: you cannot sell capacity you do not have. The gap is the argument for the business model — an exchange lets them sell other people's capacity too — rather than a number that converts to cash.

06How the money is made

Three tolls on the same hour of computing.

Exascale charges at three points on the same chain, and never buys the chip. The third toll is the smallest percentage and becomes the largest business.

Sell side · GPU cloud 30% Of what we sell for a data centre

We run the software that lets an operator rent out idle racks. It costs them nothing; they keep 70% and stop having to sell their own capacity.

Buy side · AI platform 5–25× Markup on raw computing cost

One key gives a customer 500+ AI models. We serve them on capacity we already meter, so the gap between the retail price and the cost underneath is ours.

The venue · exchange 1.8% Of every trade that crosses the book

Charged to the side that takes a price; the side that posts one is rebated. This is the smallest rate and, by year five, roughly two-thirds of all revenue.

The exchange fee is the one to understand. At 1.8%, a million dollars of computing power changing hands earns Exascale $18,000. That sounds modest until you notice what flows through this market: $193.7 billion of AI chips were sold in a single year,2 and every hour of that gets resold bilaterally today. At 1.8%, the fees sitting under that flow come to about $3.5 billion a year — and nobody collects them, because no exchange exists.

Why an exchange is worth more than a broker

There are brokers in this market already — companies that match a buyer to a named seller at a private price. All of them together are worth about $31.8 billion. The two commodity exchanges that actually print a price, CME and ICE, are worth about $184 billion between them.9

That difference — matching versus pricing — is the whole thesis. A broker is useful. An exchange becomes infrastructure that everyone else has to reference.

07Margins

Low cost, high margin — and here is exactly why.

The company's plan shows a 96% gross margin and profitability in year one, at a 71% operating margin rising to 86% by year five.OURS Those are extraordinary numbers, and the reason for them is simple and real: Exascale does not own the chips.

The expensive, depreciating hardware belongs to the data centres. Exascale supplies software and a marketplace. Its plan runs one control computer per thousand chips managed — fifteen of them in year one. Once that software exists, the ten-thousandth trade costs a fraction of a penny to process. There is no extra chip to buy, no extra warehouse to heat.

This is why exchanges as a category are among the most profitable businesses that exist, and why they keep earning long after excitement in the thing they trade has faded. The London Metal Exchange never needed copper to be interesting. It needed copper to be traded.

Two questions to put to the founders before you wire anything

1. The 66 researchers are unsalaried. The deck states in a footnote that its 66 PhD and MSc researchers "take no salary and build with AI agents", and that salarying them would add about $16 million a year. That is a real cost the plan does not carry. On year-one revenue of $36 million, adding it would take operating profit from roughly $26 million to roughly $10 million — still profitable, but a materially different company. Ask how long that arrangement holds and what happens when it does not.

2. Overheads look very thin at scale. The plan carries $3.3 million of general and administrative cost in year five — against $6.29 billion of revenue, a regulated exchange, offices on three continents and 1,373 sales staff. Sales costs are budgeted separately and generously, so this is a definitional choice rather than an obvious error, but $3.3 million of audit, insurance, legal and premises for a business of that size deserves a direct question.

The honest reading of that grid

Every cell above is a projection multiplied by an assumption and divided by a second assumption. The arithmetic is sound — I checked it — but arithmetic is not the question. All the risk sits in the first number: $6.29 billion of revenue in year five, from a company that has not yet cleared a single trade.

Note also that the grid has no zero in it. A pre-revenue, pre-licence venue has a substantial chance of returning nothing at all, and a grid whose worst case is 104× is not showing you the real range. The ladder below adds the missing rung.

What each outcome requires

0× The book never gets liquid

The LOIs do not convert, or the licences do not arrive, or traders never show up in enough numbers for a real price to form. This is the most likely single outcome for any new exchange, and it is absent from the company's grid. Assume a substantial chance of it.

~104× A real but early exit

Year three, $712 million of revenue, sold at the low end of the range. Requires the LOIs to convert and the exchange to be genuinely working — but not to have won the market.

~389× The established venue

Year four, $2.13 billion of revenue at 25 times. Requires Exascale to be the place a meaningful share of the world's computing power is priced.

For scale: that is more than the combined value of CME and ICE today — the two largest commodity exchanges on earth, built over forty years.9 It requires roughly $711 billion of computing power to trade across the book in one year, which is 3.7 times the entire world market for AI chips today. The company's answer is that financial contracts trade at a multiple of the physical goods behind them — about ten paper barrels for every real barrel of oil — and that four financial dollars per physical dollar is conservative by that standard. That is a fair argument. It is also the single largest assumption in the entire case.

09Risks

What could go wrong, stated plainly.

A deck without this page is a sales brochure. These are the things that would actually cost you your money, roughly in the order they worry me.

Letters of intent are not ordersMost serious

The $1.34 billion is the centrepiece of the case and it is non-binding. Conversion rates from LOI to paid contract vary enormously and nobody here has a track record to judge it against. Ask what share of the 214 accounts has converted to date, and on what terms.

Nobody trades on an empty marketMost serious

An exchange with no participants has no price, and without a price there is no reason to join. Every venue in history has faced this and most failed it. No trade has yet crossed this book.

The licences are not in handMost serious

$1 million of the raise is earmarked for registering as a designated contract market, money-transmitter licences and a surety bond for holding client dollars. Until that is done the exchange cannot legally clear at scale. Regulatory timetables slip.

The cost base depends on unpaid researchersMaterial

66 of the 81 staff take no salary. Paying them would cost about $16 million a year and would materially change the margin story that makes this a "low cost, high profit" business.

The price gap could close on its ownMaterial

The 4.7× spread is the reason to exist. If supply catches up and prices converge without an exchange, the need for one weakens considerably.

Big players may keep capacity in-houseMaterial

If the largest operators build for themselves and never trade, the tradable market is far smaller than the headline spending figures imply.

Three businesses, one balance sheetMaterial

The cloud and the model platform are not exchange economics — they carry operational risk and customer support that a pure venue does not. Running three businesses at once is harder than running one, and the plan assumes all three work.

The one milestone that decides whether this worked

Not revenue, not headcount, not another letter of intent. It is the first month in which a meaningful volume of computing power changes hands on the exchange at a price the exchange itself discovered. Everything before that is preparation. Everything after it is arithmetic.
