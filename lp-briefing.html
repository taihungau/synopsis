04The problemLive

Demand multiplies. Supply merely adds.

Demand for these machines grew seven times over in a single year. The supply of places to put them grew 36%. That is the whole problem in two numbers.

Why more cannot simply be built

To add capacity you must…Wait
Be allocated the machines3–6 months
Put up the building1–2 years
Get a power transformer2–4 years
Get connected to the grid5–10 years

Money does not shorten these queues. The United States is also short 439,000 building workers,7 and only 1.4% of existing space is free.4

The everyday version

Seven times as many people turned up wanting a table. The restaurant added a third more chairs. Building a bigger restaurant takes five to ten years, mostly waiting for the electricity.

05Why nowLive

Same machine, same day, four times the price.

Fifteen sellers were asked the price of the identical machine on one day. The cheapest said $1.49 an hour. The dearest said $6.98. Petrol does not work like this, because petrol has a market.

Why a marketplace appears now and not ten years ago Three things had to become true together, and they just have. The thing being sold became standard, so one hour is much like another. Lots of different people started selling it, rather than one or two. And the price started lurching about — it fell 79%, then rose 38% in five months.8 When a price moves like that, people want a way to lock it in. Nobody could. That is what builds an exchange.

The everyday version A stock exchange does not own any companies. A commodities exchange does not own any oil. They own the place where buying and selling happens, and they take a fraction of a percent every time it does. That is the whole business, and it is a very good one.

08How a trade worksLive

Buyers bid. Sellers offer. The middle matches them.

What you are looking at This is an order book, and it is how every stock exchange on earth works. Buyers queue up on the left with the most they will pay. Sellers queue on the right with the least they will accept. When the two meet, a trade happens and the price is published for everyone. Exascale takes 1.8% and never touches the machine.

12What is actually traded

You trade the ticket, not the machine.

The most technical slide in the original, and the idea underneath it is two hundred years old.

One · the ticket

You cannot run a market in “an hour on Bob’s particular machine.” So you sell a ticket for one hour of a type of machine, from nobody in particular. Metal markets have done exactly this for centuries: you trade a warrant for a tonne of copper, not a named pallet in a named shed. Tickets being interchangeable is what makes a market deep.

Two · one wallet

A buyer puts dollars into one account and decides later whether to spend them on raw machine time or on finished answers. Sellers are paid in dollars. Because there is one currency behind everything, it is one set of books rather than two.

Three · the rate between them

A finished answer is just machine time already used. So the ratio between the two prices is a number every AI business is exposed to and nobody publishes. One company disclosed its own: $87,072 of machine time a day produced $562,027 of answers — 6.5 times.10

Why three sides and not two A marketplace has two sides: people who want the thing, and people who have it. An exchange has a third — traders, who never want a machine at all and are only there to profit from the price moving. They sound like parasites. They are not: they are what makes a market deep enough that everyone else gets a fair price and can sell when they need to.

17How they get paid

Three tolls on the same hour of computing.

From the seller30%Of what we sell for them

The building owner keeps 70% and pays nothing for the software. They stop having to find their own customers. The maker of the machines launched the identical arrangement in July 2026.

From the buyer5–25×Markup on finished answers

A finished AI answer sells for many times the electricity and machine time behind it. Because Exascale already meters the machines, that difference is theirs rather than a fee paid to someone else.

From both, on every trade1.8%Of everything that crosses

Charged to whoever takes a price off the screen. Whoever posts a price is paid a small rebate instead — because posting prices is what creates a market, so it is rewarded rather than taxed.

The whole business in one sentence Exascale charges for finished answers, charges for raw machine time, and charges a small percentage on the gap between them — and only the third one grows as the shortage worsens. By year five the founders expect that third toll to be about two-thirds of everything they earn.

20Why it would be hard to copy

Each piece feeds the next.

The three products are not three businesses sharing an office. Each creates the conditions the next one needs.

The honest caveat This is an argument, not a fact. It depends on the loop actually turning, and today it has not turned once, because nothing has traded. A moat that exists only on a diagram is a hypothesis.

23What has to be true

Computing becomes something you trade. Someone prints the price.

NothingThe market never gets going

The signed letters do not turn into orders, or the licences do not arrive, or too few traders show up for a real price to form. For a marketplace that has never traded and is not yet licensed, this is the single most likely outcome — and it appears nowhere in the founders’ own figures.

Sold in year four. Needs Exascale to be where a real share of the world’s computing is priced.

~1,147×It becomes the oil market of computing

Sold in year five for about $157 billion — more than the world’s two largest commodity exchanges put together, which took forty years to build.9 It needs $711 billion of computing to change hands there in one year, over three times today’s entire world market. The founders’ answer is that people always trade far more paper than goods — roughly ten paper barrels for every real barrel of oil — so four to one is modest. It is a fair argument.
