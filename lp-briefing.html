03Vocabulary

The only six words you need.

The rest of this deck avoids jargon. These six are unavoidable, so here they are once.

GPU

A specialised computer chip. The engine AI runs on — a very expensive, very fast electric motor.

GPU-hour

One chip running for one hour. The unit that gets bought and sold, the way oil is sold by the barrel.

Exchange

A marketplace with one public price where buyers and sellers meet. A stock exchange, but for computing power.

Take rate

The small percentage the marketplace keeps from each transaction. How we get paid.

Basis point

One hundredth of one percent. "180 basis points" means 1.8%. Bankers count this way; it means nothing more.

LOI

Letter of intent. A signed statement that a customer intends to buy. Serious, but not yet an order.

07Business model

Three tolls on the same hour of computing.

Exascale charges at three points on one chain, and never buys the chip. The third toll is the smallest percentage and becomes the largest business.

Sell side · GPU cloud 30% Of what we sell for a data centre

We run the software that lets an operator rent out idle racks. It costs them nothing; they keep 70% and stop having to sell their own capacity.

Buy side · AI platform 5–25× Markup on raw computing cost

One key gives a customer 500+ AI models. We serve them on capacity we already meter, so the gap between retail price and cost underneath is ours.

The venue · exchange 1.8% Of every trade that crosses the book

Charged to the side that takes a price; the side that posts one is rebated. Smallest rate, and by year five roughly two-thirds of all revenue.

The number to hold onto

At 1.8%, a million dollars of computing power changing hands earns $18,000. $193.7bn of AI chips sold in a single year,2 every hour of it resold bilaterally — about $3.5bn a year of fees sitting there, uncollected, because no exchange exists.

Why an exchange beats a broker

Every broker in this market together is worth about $31.8bn. The two exchanges that actually print a price, CME and ICE, are worth about $184bn.9 Matching is useful; pricing becomes infrastructure everyone else references.

08Margins

Low cost, high margin — and exactly why.

The plan shows a 96% gross margin and profitability in year one, at a 71% operating margin rising to 86% by year five.OURS The reason is simple and real: Exascale does not own the chips. The expensive, depreciating hardware belongs to the data centres. Once the software exists, the ten-thousandth trade costs a fraction of a penny. No extra chip to buy, no extra warehouse to heat.

Two questions to put to the founders before wiring anything

1. The 66 researchers are unsalaried. The full deck states in a footnote that its 66 PhD and MSc researchers take no salary, and that paying them would add about $16m a year. On year-one revenue of $36m, that moves operating profit from roughly $26m to roughly $10m — still profitable, but a materially different company. Ask how long the arrangement holds.

2. Overheads look thin at scale. The plan carries $3.3m of general and administrative cost in year five, against $6.29bn of revenue, a regulated exchange, three offices and 1,373 sales staff. Sales costs are budgeted separately, so this is a definitional choice rather than a clear error — but it deserves a direct question.

11What has to be true

Read this ladder from the bottom up.

Each rung is not a prediction. It is a condition — what the world would have to do for that outcome to happen.

0×The book never gets liquid

The LOIs do not convert, or the licences do not arrive, or traders never appear in enough numbers for a real price to form. This is the most likely single outcome for any new exchange, and it is absent from the company's own grid.

~104×A real but early exit

Year three, $712m of revenue, sold at the low end. Requires the LOIs to convert and the exchange to work — but not to have won the market.

~389×The established venue

Year four, $2.13bn of revenue at 25 times. Requires Exascale to be where a meaningful share of the world's computing power is priced.

~1,147×Exascale becomes the CME of compute

Year five, $6.29bn of revenue, valued at $157bn — more than CME and ICE combined today, the two largest commodity exchanges on earth, built over forty years.9 It needs $711bn of computing power crossing the book in a year, 3.7 times the entire world market for AI chips. The company's answer is that financial contracts trade at a multiple of the goods behind them — about ten paper barrels per real barrel of oil — and four per physical dollar is conservative by that standard. A fair argument, and the single largest assumption in the case.

12Risks

What could go wrong, stated plainly.

A deck without this slide is a sales brochure. These are the things that would actually cost you your money.

Letters of intent are not ordersMost serious

The $1.34bn centrepiece is non-binding. Conversion rates vary enormously and there is no track record to judge against. Ask what share of the 214 accounts has converted, and on what terms.

Nobody trades on an empty marketMost serious

An exchange with no participants has no price, and without a price there is no reason to join. Every venue in history has faced this; most failed it. Nothing has crossed this book yet.

The licences are not in handMost serious

$1m of the raise is for exchange registration, money-transmitter licences and a client-money bond. Until that lands the venue cannot legally clear at scale, and regulatory timetables slip.

The cost base depends on unpaid researchersMaterial

66 of 81 staff take no salary. Paying them would cost about $16m a year and would materially change the margin story that makes this a low-cost, high-profit business.

The price gap could close on its ownMaterial

The 4.7× spread is the reason to exist. If supply catches up and prices converge without an exchange, the need for one weakens considerably.

Three businesses, one balance sheetMaterial

The cloud and the model platform are not exchange economics — they carry operational risk a pure venue does not. The plan assumes all three work at once.

Not revenue, not headcount, not another letter of intent. It is the first month in which a meaningful volume of computing power changes hands on the exchange at a price the exchange itself discovered. Everything before that is preparation; everything after is arithmetic.
