Exascale / Briefing for limited partners

The tollbooth on the road everyone is paving.

The world is spending hundreds of billions of dollars a year building computers to run artificial intelligence. There is still no marketplace where that computing power is bought and sold at one public price. We are building it. This document explains, in plain English, what that business is, how it earns money, and what would have to happen for it to be worth a great deal.

01The whole thing in six sentences

If you read nothing else, read this page.

1. Artificial intelligence runs on specialised computer chips called GPUs. Every chatbot, every self-driving car, every AI feature in every product needs them, by the thousand, running day and night.

2. The world is short of them, and is spending enormous sums to fix that. The four biggest technology companies alone plan to spend roughly $725 billion on this in 2026 — up 77% in a single year.3

3. Today these chips are rented privately, one negotiation at a time. There is no public price. The same chip, doing the same job, rents for anywhere between $1.49 and $6.98 an hour depending on who you happen to ask.5

4. That is what a market looks like before it has an exchange. Oil looked like that. Electricity looked like that. Grain looked like that. In each case somebody built the exchange, and the exchange became more durable and more profitable than most of the companies trading on it.

5. Exascale is that exchange for computing power. We do not buy chips. We do not own buildings. We run the marketplace where owners and users meet, and we keep a small percentage of everything that changes hands.

6. We are raising $5 million to get from a working system to a licensed, live venue with real trading on it. That is the whole proposition.

Please read this before the return figures

You will not find a promised return multiple in this document. Any specific multiple — 10×, 100×, 1,000× — stated to a prospective investor is a forecast, and a forecast nobody can support is both a legal exposure for us and, in our experience, the fastest way to lose a serious investor's attention.

What section 08 does instead is show you the arithmetic in reverse: what would have to be true for each level of return, so you can judge for yourself how likely each one is. We think that is a more useful page, and a more confident one.

02Five words you will hear

The only vocabulary you need.

The rest of this document avoids jargon. These five terms are unavoidable, so here they are once, in ordinary language.

GPU
A specialised computer chip. The engine that artificial intelligence runs on. Think of it as a very expensive, very fast electric motor.
GPU-hour
One chip, running for one hour. The unit that gets bought and sold, the way oil is sold by the barrel.
Exchange
A marketplace with one public price, where buyers and sellers meet. A stock exchange, but for computing power.
Take rate
The small percentage the marketplace keeps from each transaction. How we get paid.
Data centre
The warehouse full of chips. Someone has to own the building and pay the electricity bill. That someone is not us.
03Demand

The demand side is not in question.

This is the one part of the story that requires no persuasion. The money is already being spent, publicly, by companies that must disclose it.

Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Meta have each told their own shareholders what they intend to spend on this in 2026. Added together it comes to roughly $725 billion, up from $410 billion the year before.3 A second outlet puts the figure near $700 billion.2 These are not forecasts by us; they are the companies' own guidance.

The electricity tells the same story from another direction. The International Energy Agency — a government body, not an industry group — reports that data centres used 415 terawatt-hours of electricity in 2024, about 1.5% of all the electricity in the world,6 and expects that to more than double by 2030, to roughly 945 terawatt-hours: slightly more than the entire country of Japan uses today.7

Why we show you the power bill instead of a market forecast

Anyone can commission a chart showing a market growing to a trillion dollars. Electricity consumption is measured by utilities and reported by a government agency. It is the least flattering and most reliable number available, which is exactly why it is here.

04Supply

The supply side is stuck, and stuck in a useful way.

Building this capacity is slow and physical. You need the chips, which are made by essentially one company. You need a building. Above all you need electricity, and in the quantities involved that means a connection to the power grid, which in most countries now takes years to obtain.

Supply is also politically fragile. In January 2026 the rules governing which advanced chips may be exported where were rewritten, moving the top chips to case-by-case licensing and applying a 25% tariff to some imports.8 In May, transfers to China-headquartered companies operating outside China were restricted too.9

The result is capacity that is expensive, unevenly distributed, and frequently in the wrong place at the wrong time. Some operator always has racks sitting idle this week while some lab elsewhere is desperate for them. Today those two parties have no reliable way to find each other.

05The gap

The same chip, the same hour, at five times the price.

This single fact is the reason the company exists. If you remember one chart from this document, remember this one.

A survey of more than fifteen providers found the identical chip — an NVIDIA H100 — advertised at anywhere from $1.49 to $6.98 per hour.5 That is a 4.7-fold difference for a product with no meaningful variation. Specialist providers cluster between $1.50 and $2.50; the large general-purpose clouds sit in the mid-single digits; capacity sold at short notice has changed hands near $1.03.4

In any other commodity this would be intolerable. You cannot buy petrol at five times your neighbour's price, because there is a public market price and everyone can see it. Computing power has no such price. Creating it is the business.

06The business model

We are the marketplace, not the merchandise.

We never buy a chip, never sign an electricity contract, never own a building. We match the people who do with the people who need them, and keep a small slice.

Sellers Data centres with idle racks

They have capacity sitting unused this week. Today it earns nothing. On our venue they list it and it earns something.

Exascale 1.5–2.25% of everything that changes hands
Buyers AI companies and labs

They need capacity now, at a fair price, without signing a year-long contract for capacity they may not use.

Our fees are published, not negotiated in the dark. The headline rate is 180 basis points — 1.80% — charged to the party who takes a price off the board, and 45 basis points (0.45%) to the party who posted it. On a trade of $1,000,000 that is $18,000 from one side and $4,500 from the other.

$1,000,000 traded × 2.25% = $22,500 to us At the full published schedule. We model $15,000 — a 1.5% blended rate — because the largest traders are rebated to zero to attract them, and they will be a large share of early volume.

Everything else is free: no charge to provision, no charge to move data out, no charge to hold a balance, no minimum commitment. Those fees are how the existing providers lock customers in, and refusing to charge them is a large part of why anyone would switch.

07Margins

Why this shape of business earns so well.

Building the system is expensive. Running it is close to free. Once the software that matches buyers to sellers exists, the ten-thousandth trade costs essentially nothing to process — a fraction of a penny of computer time. There is no extra chip to buy, no extra warehouse to heat, no extra lorry to send.

That is why exchanges, as a category, are among the most profitable businesses that exist, and why they tend to keep earning long after the excitement in whatever they trade has faded. The London Metal Exchange did not need copper to be interesting. It needed copper to be traded.

An honest qualification you should press us on

The "low cost, high margin" description is true of the exchange. It is not true of owning and renting out chips yourself — that is a capital-hungry business with thin margins, where you buy depreciating hardware and resell electricity.

Exascale's own materials describe it as both an exchange and a compute provider. If we do both, the blended margin will be materially lower than the exchange alone, and the capital requirement materially higher. Ask us which of the two this $5 million is funding. It is the right question, and the answer determines what kind of company you are buying into.

08Returns

What would have to be true to make money here.

Read this ladder from the bottom up. Each rung is not a prediction. It is a condition — the thing the world would have to do for that outcome to happen.

The arithmetic below assumes $5 million buys 20% of the company today, and that later fundraising dilutes that to roughly 8% by the time there is an exit. Both assumptions are ordinary for a company at this stage, and both are stated so you can vary them.

0× The venue never gets liquid

We never attract enough traders for a real price to form, or we fail to obtain the licences a venue holding client money needs. This is the single most likely outcome for any new exchange, and you should assume a substantial chance of it. Investors lose the full $5 million.

~4× A working niche venue

$2 billion traded a year, $30 million of revenue, sold for eight times revenue — about $240 million. An 8% stake returns roughly $19 million. A good outcome by most standards, and a disappointing one for a venture fund.

~48× The established venue

$20 billion traded a year, $300 million of revenue, valued at ten times revenue — about $3 billion. An 8% stake returns roughly $240 million. This is the outcome the business is actually designed for.

~1,000× Exascale becomes the CME of compute

To return $5 billion on $5 million, an 8% stake must be worth $5 billion — so the company must be worth roughly $67 billion. At twelve times revenue that means about $5.6 billion of annual revenue, which at our 1.5% fee means roughly $370 billion of computing power changing hands on our venue every year. For scale: that would make Exascale about as valuable as CME Group or Intercontinental Exchange — the two largest derivatives exchanges on earth, each built over decades.

Revenue multiples used here are in the range mature exchange businesses have historically traded at. They are assumptions, not guarantees, and a downturn compresses them for everyone.

So is the top rung possible?

It is not absurd, and that is the strongest honest claim available. $370 billion of annual trading sounds enormous until you set it beside the $725 billion a year that four companies alone are spending to build this capacity.3 The market is already that big. The question is not whether the volume exists — it plainly does — but whether it ever moves onto an open venue instead of staying inside private contracts.

That is the bet you would be making. Not a bet on artificial intelligence, which is already settled. A bet on market structure — that computing power follows oil, gas, electricity, grain and metals onto an exchange, and that we are the one that gets built.

09Risks

What could go wrong, stated plainly.

A deck without this section is a sales brochure. These are the things that would actually cost you your money, roughly in order of how much they worry us.

Nobody trades on an empty marketMost serious

An exchange with no participants has no price, and without a price there is no reason to join. Every venue in history has faced this and most have failed it. Solving it usually costs money — paying traders to be there early — and that cost is real.

We are not licensed yetMost serious

A venue that holds client money and lists forward contracts is a regulated financial business in every market that matters. We do not yet hold those permissions. Our own public materials deliberately refuse to claim we are operating, because we are not.

The price gap could close on its ownMaterial

The 4.7× spread is our reason to exist. If supply catches up with demand and prices converge without us, the need for a venue weakens considerably.

The biggest players may simply keep it in-houseMaterial

If a handful of very large companies build capacity solely for themselves and never trade it, the tradable market is far smaller than the headline spending figures suggest.

Politics moves the chips aroundMaterial

Export controls were rewritten twice in 2026 alone.89 Rules that change which chips may cross which border change which markets we can serve, at short notice and without warning.

Adoption is not a straight lineMaterial

Klarna replaced the work of 700 support agents with AI and reported a $40 million profit improvement.10 Fifteen months later it began hiring people back, conceding the result was lower quality.11 Both facts are in this document deliberately.

10The ask

$5 million, and what it is for.

The money takes Exascale from a built system to a licensed venue with real trading on it. Broadly: the regulatory permissions and the legal work around client money; the clearing and settlement machinery that lets strangers trade safely with each other; and the cost of attracting the first traders, without whom nothing else matters.

The single milestone that determines whether this was a good investment is not revenue and not headcount. It is the first month in which a meaningful volume of computing power changes hands on the venue at a price the venue itself discovered. Everything before that is preparation; everything after it is arithmetic.

Three questions we would ask if we were you

1. Is this $5 million funding the exchange, the compute business, or both? (See the qualification in section 07.)

2. Which licences, in which jurisdiction, and what is the realistic timetable and cost of obtaining them?

3. Who are the first ten participants, and what have they actually committed to — in writing, not in conversation?
