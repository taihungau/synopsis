02Purpose

Compute is becoming the world’s next major commodity.

Artificial intelligence consumes compute the way industry consumes energy. But compute is not yet traded as a commodity is traded. It is fragmented across providers, priced in private contracts, hard to compare between sellers, impossible to hedge, and difficult to lend against.

The entire company in four words
OnePrice

A continuous, public reference price for a standardised unit of compute.

TwoTrade

Spot and forward markets where that price is discovered rather than negotiated.

ThreeHedge

Derivatives that let buyers and operators transfer compute price risk.

FourFinance

Credit written against compute capacity, underwritten on an observable price.

The thesis in one line Every major commodity has followed the same progression — physical asset, then standardised price, then spot market, then derivatives, then financing, then a global financial market. Compute is early in that sequence and moving through it unusually fast. Exascale exists to build the layer it is moving into.

03Problem

The world’s most important new commodity has no mature market.

An AI company asks “What will one GPU-hour cost me next year?” No universal answer. Every provider quotes privately.
An operator asks “What are my GPUs worth if compute prices fall?” No liquid hedge. The exposure cannot be transferred.
A lender asks “How do I underwrite $1B of GPU infrastructure?” Limited price discovery. No forward curve to lend against.
An investor asks “How do I get exposure to the growth of compute?” No standardised asset class. Only equity proxies.

The precise gap Compute already has everything a commodity market needs except the market itself: real supply, real demand, real scarcity and real price volatility. Published rates for an identical H100 span $1.49 to $6.98 per hour on the same day across fifteen providers. A barrel of Brent moves about a hundredth of a percent between sellers, because oil has had an exchange since 1988.

05Why now

Three markets are converging.

I AI demand is driving an unprecedented buildout. JLL projects global data-centre capacity nearly doubling to around 200 GW by 2030, requiring up to $3 trillion of investment, with AI rising from a quarter of capacity to half of it. PwC puts cumulative AI infrastructure capex at $31.6 trillion through 2050 — roughly $800bn a year today, $1.8tn a year by 2050.
II Compute now behaves like a scarce economic input, not IT hardware. Prices move on supply shocks, not product cycles: the H100 one-year rate fell 79%, then rose 38% in five months. Capacity cannot respond — a grid connection takes five to ten years, and vacancy across primary markets is 1.4%.
III Financial markets have already arrived. On 11 August 2026 CME Group and Silicon Data announced two compute futures contracts listing on NYMEX on 5 October 2026, subject to regulatory review: cash-settled futures on the Silicon Data H100 and B200 rental indices, each contract one month of GPU rental.

How to read CME’s entry Not as competition to be explained away. As the validation event. The correct claim is not “nobody is doing this” — that is now demonstrably false — but “the world’s largest derivatives exchange has made the bet that compute is a tradable commodity, and the infrastructure layer around it is still unbuilt.” A single cash-settled futures contract is the first brick, not the building.

The honest position Exascale does not have this category to itself, and should not claim to. CME has validated the asset class and will own the first listed contract. What remains unbuilt is everything around it: physical spot delivery, continuous exposure, capacity financing, and the settlement layer that connects a financial contract to an actual running machine. CME is a futures exchange; it does not deliver compute. That is the opening.

08Why Exascale wins

The exchange becomes the reference point for compute.

Why the loop is the defensible thing Each stage is the precondition for the next, and the last returns to the first. Liquidity produces a trustworthy benchmark; a benchmark makes derivatives writable; derivatives make capacity financeable; financing and trading together generate the transaction data that makes the benchmark better still. A competitor can copy any single stage. None of them works alone, and the venue that assembles the whole loop becomes the price everyone else quotes against — which is the position ICE holds in oil and CME holds in rates.

Index & dataThe benchmark

The settlement price, the curve and the transaction record. Licensed to everyone, including competitors.

What the stack is for This is what separates market infrastructure from a GPU marketplace. A marketplace sells hours. An exchange sells hours, the right to buy hours later, exposure to their price, protection against it moving, capital against the machines that produce them, and the number everyone uses to value all of it. CME’s October listing validates the third and fourth rows of this table; the first, second, fifth and sixth remain open.

The distinction that decides this slide Spot and derivatives are different businesses with take rates three orders of magnitude apart. A physical marketplace earns 100–180 bp because it delivers a good; a futures exchange earns a fraction of a basis point because it transfers risk at enormous scale. $30T of notional at listed-futures economics is revenue in the tens of millions, not $300B. The large number comes from the spot layer, the large volume from the derivatives layer, and any model that multiplies one by the other is wrong. Treat every figure on this slide as illustrative economics at scale, never as forecast.
